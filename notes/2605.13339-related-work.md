# Where Gilg et al. (2605.13339) sits in the field

Notes on *Probing Persona-Dependent Preferences in Language Models* — Oscar Gilg,
Pierre Beckmann, Daniel Paleka, Patrick Butlin. arXiv 2605.13339v2, 18 May 2026.
Source PDF and text in `sources/`.

## Chronology — how the field got here

| when | what | why it matters here |
|---|---|---|
| 2020 | Ravfogel et al., iterated nullspace projection | The way to remove a linear direction and see what breaks. Gilg et al.'s App. I runs on it. |
| 2022 | janus, *Simulators*; Andreas, *LMs as agent models* | Says an LLM runs characters, not one self. Argument, no measurement. |
| 2023 | Shanahan, McDonell & Reynolds, *Role play with LLMs* (Nature) | Same claim in a journal. Still no measurement. |
| 2024 | Arditi et al., refusal is one direction | The template the whole field copies: one behaviour, one linear direction, decode it and steer it. |
| 2024 | Marks & Tegmark, geometry of truth; MacDiarmid et al., probes catch sleeper agents | Same template turned to safety. Probes as monitors. |
| Feb 2025 | Mazeika et al., *Utility engineering* | Fits Thurstonian utilities to LLM forced choices. Coherence grows with scale. The behavioural parent of this paper. |
| 2025 | Zhao et al., harmfulness separate from refusal; Goldowsky-Dill et al., deception probes | The direction zoo fills up. |
| Jul 2025 | Chen et al., *Persona vectors* | Traits — evil, sycophancy — as directions you can monitor and steer. Closest method to this paper. |
| Sep 2025 | Wang et al., *False sense of security* (2509.03888) | Probes learn instructional patterns and trigger words, not harmfulness, and generalise poorly. The first big crack in the template. |
| Oct 2025 | Lu, Song & Wang, valence-assent axis | One axis carrying "what is good" and "what is true" together. Closest in spirit. |
| Jan 2026 | Lu et al., *The Assistant Axis*; Lampinen et al., representations change over a conversation | The default persona gets its own direction. Directions stop being stable objects. |
| Apr 2026 | Sofroniew et al., emotion concepts | 171 emotion directions in one model, causally shaping generation. |
| 8 May 2026 | Yamin et al. (2605.08556) | Revealed preferences for LLMs, from the outside. Behavioural sibling. |
| **18 May 2026** | **Gilg et al. (2605.13339)** | **First to read a direction off revealed choice, and first to measure persona-sharing.** |
| 28 May 2026 | Han, Chalmers & Izmailov (2605.30232) | RL recruits a pre-existing valence axis that then moves unrelated behaviour. Same answer, from RL. |
| Jun 2026 | Shared valence axis vs human EEG (2606.00129) | The axis lines up with 123 people's EEG. |
| Jul 2026 | Emergent misalignment recruits a pre-existing persona subspace (2607.21356) | Same answer again, from misalignment. |

Read the last four rows together. Three groups, three methods — choice, reward,
fine-tuning damage — converge inside four months on one claim: there is an
evaluative axis already in the model, and new behaviour gets attached to it
rather than building its own. That convergence is the reason to trust the
direction of travel, and it is stronger evidence than any one of the three
papers.

## What the paper does

They fit utilities to a model's own pairwise task choices, then train a ridge
probe on residual-stream activations at the end-of-turn token to predict those
utilities. Gemma-3-27B and Qwen-3.5-122B. Two claims:

1. The direction is evaluative, not descriptive. Steering it swings pairwise
   choice from 1% to 99% on Gemma. It separates true from false statements. Its
   harm-vs-benign readout flips sign under an evil system prompt, while a
   text-encoder probe on the same conversation does not flip.
2. The direction is shared across personas. A probe trained on the Assistant
   predicts six other personas' utilities better than a baseline that just
   copies Assistant utilities, and steers all of them. Under evil, pushing the
   direction makes the model more evil; under the Assistant it does nothing to
   evilness.

They are careful about what they did not show. Steering works on Gemma only; the
Qwen pilot came back negative. They found no persona-independent attractor. They
make an existence claim about the direction, not a uniqueness claim.

## The three lines this joins

**Behaviour.** Utility engineering (Mazeika et al. 2025) measures LLM
preferences from forced choices and fits a Thurstonian utility. Black box. Gilg
et al. take its pipeline whole and use the utilities as probe targets.

**Representation.** Refusal is one direction (Arditi et al. 2024). Persona
traits are directions (Chen et al. 2025). The default Assistant is a direction
(Lu et al. 2026). Emotion concepts are directions that shape generation
(Sofroniew et al. 2026). A valence-assent axis carries "what is good" and "what
is true" together (Lu, Song and Wang 2025). Every one of these anchors its
direction on a label, a trait name, or a stated judgment.

**Persona theory.** Simulators (janus 2022), role-play (Shanahan et al. 2023),
agent models (Andreas 2022), the persona selection model (Marks et al. 2026).
These ask whether one machine wears many masks. Until now the question was
argued, not measured.

**The move.** Gilg et al. anchor the direction in *revealed choice* rather than
in a label, and then use it to answer the persona-theory question with numbers.
That join is the paper's real contribution. The welfare framing is a rider.

## Work they cite, grouped

- Linear directions: Arditi et al. 2024 (refusal); Marks and Tegmark 2024
  (truth); Zhao et al. 2025 (harmfulness separate from refusal); Ravfogel et al.
  2020 (nullspace).
- Evaluative and affective: Sofroniew et al. 2026; Lu, Song and Wang 2025
  (valence-assent axis); Carruthers 2018 (valence and value, philosophy).
- Persona: Chen et al. 2025 (persona vectors); Lu et al. 2026 (assistant axis);
  Lampinen et al. 2026 (linear representations shift over a conversation);
  Beckmann and Butlin 2026; Maiya et al. 2025 (character training); Chalmers
  2026 (Aura); Betley et al. 2026 (emergent misalignment).
- Preference measurement: Mazeika et al. 2025; Gu et al. 2025 (stated vs
  revealed); Chiu et al. 2025 (AIRiskDilemmas); Keeling et al. 2024 (stipulated
  pain and pleasure); Ensign et al. 2025 (bail preferences); Huang et al. 2025
  (values in the wild); Khan et al. 2025 (elicitation format shapes the answer).
- Probes for safety: Goldowsky-Dill et al. 2025; MacDiarmid et al. 2024;
  Haralambiev 2026 (probes catch liars but miss fanatics).
- Welfare: Long et al. 2024; Butlin 2026.

## Work they miss

Ordered by how much it should change the paper.

1. **Han, Chalmers and Izmailov 2026, "How's it going? Reinforcement learning in
   language models recruits a functional welfare axis"** (arXiv 2605.30232).
   They RL models in a neutral maze, pull vectors for high- and low-reward
   trajectories, and find those vectors land on a pre-existing valence axis that
   then moves sentiment, confidence, backtracking and refusal in unrelated
   settings. Same claim shape as Gilg et al. — one evaluative axis, recruited
   rather than built, with reach beyond the task it was read from — arrived at
   from the RL side rather than the choice side. It also speaks straight to
   their welfare section. Posted ten days after their v2, so the omission is
   fair, but any follow-up must place itself against both.

2. **Yamin, Tang, Horvitz and Wilder 2026, "Can Revealed Preferences Clarify LLM
   Alignment and Steering?"** (arXiv 2605.08556). Fits a discrete-choice model
   to recover the cost function that rationalises an LLM's decisions, then asks
   whether the model can verbalise that policy and whether prompting steers it.
   The behavioural sibling of Gilg et al., ten days earlier. They test whether
   prompting steers revealed policy; Gilg et al. test whether a vector does.

3. **The probe-generalisation literature.** Gilg et al. present persona-shift as
   a fresh failure mode for white-box safety. The failure itself is documented:
   Wang et al., "False Sense of Security" (arXiv 2509.03888), show probes learn
   instructional patterns and trigger words rather than harmfulness and so
   generalise poorly;
   "Pressure-Testing Deception Probes" (arXiv 2605.27958) names a probe
   fragility gap. What Gilg et al. add is a *cause* — the active persona, not
   surface form or adversarial pressure. Say it that way and the claim is
   stronger, not weaker.

4. **Reward-model interpretability.** The reward side has its own value-direction
   work that neither field cites across: reward-lens (arXiv 2604.26130) projects
   the residual stream onto the reward direction layer by layer; SAE work on
   preference instability in reward models (arXiv 2605.16339). If a model's
   internal preference vector and its reward model's direction agree, that is a
   result. Nobody has checked.

5. **Value coding in neuroscience.** The paper's question — do different personas
   run on one preference machine — is the common-currency question, asked of a
   transformer. Levy and Glimcher 2012, "The root of all value: a neural common
   currency for choice," is the canonical statement; vmPFC and OFC carry a scale
   on which unlike rewards compare. The complications match the paper's
   complications: value coding is part modality-general and part
   modality-specific, and vmPFC builds a cognitive map along choice-relevant
   dimensions rather than one scalar (Cell, 2025). Gilg et al. find transfer
   across personas that is real but noisy, and no shared attractor. That is the
   same shape of answer. Naming the parallel gives the result a frame it lacks
   and a hypothesis to test: persona as *state*, with state-dependent utilities
   read off a partly shared scale.

6. **Later persona work worth tracking.** "Emergent Misalignment Recruits a
   Pre-existing Persona Subspace" (arXiv 2607.21356) and "Misalignment Has a
   Personality" (arXiv 2607.26389). Both post-date the paper. Both argue that
   misalignment reuses machinery that was already there — the recruitment story
   again.

7. **Valence geometry.** "Valence–Arousal Subspace in LLMs" (arXiv 2604.03147)
   argues the affective structure is a circular two-dimensional subspace, not an
   axis. "A Shared Valence Axis Across Modern LLMs and Human EEG" (arXiv
   2606.00129) reports the LLM valence axis pointing the same way as EEG in 123
   people. If valence is two-dimensional, a one-dimensional preference vector is
   a projection, and the residual may be where the persona-specific variance
   went.

## Verdict

The paper is a first, twice over: the first to read an evaluative direction off
revealed choices rather than off labels, and the first to measure whether
personas share preference machinery. Both firsts are narrow. The causal half
rests on one model; the second model's pilot failed. The welfare argument is a
conditional stacked on a conditional and does no work the rest of the paper
needs.

The finding likely to last is the negative one for safety: what a probe reads
depends on which persona is running, so a probe trained under the Assistant can
inverse under another persona while a content-only baseline holds steady. That
is a specific, checkable claim about where probes break, and it is more useful
than the welfare framing it is wrapped in.

The open seam: they make an existence claim, find no persona-independent
attractor, and report noisy transfer. Nobody has asked what the residual is —
whether the part of preference that does not transfer is itself structured, and
whether it is the persona-specific part that safety work actually needs.
